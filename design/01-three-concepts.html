Design proposals

Three designs for Italiano

Three complete, genuinely different ways to rebuild the app as a task-based full course — each one drawn from the evidence review, each shown screen by screen. They share an engine and disagree about everything else.

The brief, and what it rules out

You asked for a task-based full course that shows achievement instead of a streak, knows which words you know and uses that knowledge, teaches the 2,000 fondamentale words first, respects acquisition order, and is built for a Polish speaker who also speaks English. Each of those has a direct design consequence — and together they rule out most of what the current app does.

01
The streak has to go, and coverage takes its place

Hours logged correlated near-zero with actual proficiency, and a fifth of learners farmed the lowest-difficulty lessons available. A streak rewards showing up; it does not reward learning. Every concept here leads with a mastery number instead. Jiang et al. 2021

02
Production first, answer last

Repeated testing beat repeated study 80% vs 36% at one week. So no screen may show the answer before you have tried to produce it, and a wrong answer gets a hint and a second attempt before the reveal — never an instant red X with the solution. Karpicke & Roediger 2008; Li 2010

03
The word model is load-bearing, not a statistic

Input only works above ~95% known-word coverage. Since the app must generate stories and dialogues, the lexicon isn't a progress bar — it is the input to the content generator. Everything you read is built from what you already know plus a measured dose of what you don't. Nation 2006, 2007

04
Four strands, or it isn't a course

If this is the whole course, it owes you meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development. The current app does the third one well and the others barely. All three concepts build the missing three. Nation 2007

05
Stage gating is a promise, not a restriction

Italian structures emerge in a fixed order. The congiuntivo stays in what you read and hear from day one — but you are never marked wrong on it until you're developmentally ready. The app grades you only up to your stage. Pienemann 1989; Chini 2016

06
Explain in Polish categories, teach through both languages

Slavic learners reasoned about Romance grammar via their L1 in 93% of cases — after twelve years of English. So aspect gets explained through dokonany / niedokonany, not through English tenses. But cognate mappings run through both Polish and English, because the two languages unlock different words. Toth et al. 2024

07
Articles get a permanent strand

Polish has no articles, and the research says article errors survive into advanced proficiency. This is the one place the app should nag forever. Not a lesson in week two — a thread that runs the whole length of the course. Polish-L1 article studies; Chini 2016

08
Voice, on mobile, is where the effect sizes are

In the chatbot meta-analysis, mobile availability (g 0.79 vs 0.19), voice input (0.81 vs 0.43) and generative AI (0.83 vs 0.47) were the significant moderators. Speaking is also the skill every app study shows lagging. All three concepts require you to talk. Lyu, Lai & Guo 2025

What stays from the current app

The five-level A1–C1 ladder, the Leitner scheduler in srs.js, the known/learning word states in storage.js, speech playback, and the theme system are all sound and all reusable. What changes is what sits on top: the home screen's headline metric, the unit of work (a task, not a module), and the fact that content becomes generated against your lexicon rather than authored as fixed lists.

The engine all three share

The three concepts differ in metaphor, structure and feel — but they run on the same five subsystems. Pick a concept for how it feels; you get this machinery either way. This is also the part worth building first, because it is what makes any of the three possible.

1 · The lexicon — a word's life

Today storage.js stores a word as known or learning. That's too coarse to generate text from. The redesign gives every one of the 2,000 fondamentale words a state, and the state decides what the generator is allowed to do with it.

UNSEEN Not yet met. Invisible to the generator.
MET Seen in input, glossed, never recalled. Counts toward the 2–5% unknown budget.
LEARNING In the scheduler, boxes 1–2. May appear, always with a gloss available.
KNOWN Boxes 3–4. Free to use unglossed. Counts toward coverage.
SOLID Box 5, the top box — reached by recalling it correctly after a week away. Next due in 21 days. Safe to build a whole sentence out of.
The generation rule

Any text the app produces for you — a story paragraph, a dialogue line, a task prompt — must be at least 95% KNOWN or SOLID, with the remaining 5% drawn deliberately from words you're due to meet next. That is the Nation threshold applied literally: it is the difference between input and noise, and it is only computable because the app tracks the lexicon.

It also gives you something no textbook can do: the text gets harder exactly as fast as you do, and never faster.

2 · The stage gate

From the Pavia Project sequences. Your stage is inferred from what you actually produce correctly, not from which lessons you've opened. Everything above your stage still appears in what you read and hear — exposure is what eventually makes it learnable — but it is never marked wrong.

The article position on the gender ladder is where the Polish strand hooks in.

You asked for mappings rather than words, and you have two source languages to map from — which is a genuine advantage, because English and Polish unlock different Italian vocabulary. A course written for English speakers throws away half of what you already know.

Where Polish beats English, and where it betrays you

Polish wins: Italian fabbrica is a factory, and so is Polish fabryka — while English fabric is a false friend that actively misleads. Same story for lezione / lekcja, azione / akcja.

Polish betrays: colazione is breakfast, not kolacja (supper). droga is a drug, not droga (road). firma is a signature, not a company. divano is a sofa, not dywan (carpet). These get their own drill type, because they are exactly the errors this learner will make and no English-first course will ever surface them.

4 · The Polish layer

Three things run in Polish throughout, because in each case Polish is the better scaffold and English is a detour.

  • Aspect. Passato prossimo vs imperfetto is taught as dokonany vs niedokonany: przeczytałem → ho letto, czytałem → leggevo. Then the app explicitly flags the verbs where the intuition fails — być has no perfective partner, so both era and è stato collapse into był and the contrast becomes invisible.
  • Articles. A permanent strand, sequenced definite → indefinite → fused prepositions, appearing in every session forever.
  • Pronunciation. Skipped almost entirely — Polish already gives you pure vowels, a trilled r, and transparent spelling. The budget goes to the two things Polish does not prepare you for: geminates (nono / nonno) and mobile stress, since Polish stress is fixed on the penultimate syllable and Italian's is not.

5 · The achievement model

Four honest numbers replace the streak. Each concept surfaces them differently, but all four exist underneath every design.

Not because streaks don't work — they demonstrably drive engagement — but because they drive it toward the cheapest qualifying action. If you ever want a nudge, the honest version is "14 words fall out of Solid this week unless you review". That's a real consequence, not a manufactured one.

◆
Concept A

Copertura — the coverage instrument

The app is a gauge. You are filling a reservoir of 2,000 words, and a number at the top tells you what share of real spoken and written Italian you could currently decode. Everything else in the app exists to move that number.

This concept takes the single most motivating true fact in the research — that De Mauro's 2,000 fondamentale words account for about 86% of everything Italians say and write — and makes it the product's spine. The reservoir is literally drawn: 2,000 cells you can see filling up. Nothing is hidden behind a metaphor.

Why this one might be right for you

It is the most honest of the three, and the least likely to feel like a toy. If what keeps you going is watching a number you know isn't gamed, this is the design that gives you that. It's also the cheapest to build well, because it leans hardest on machinery the app already has.

What Concept A gets right, and what it costs

Right: it is the most direct expression of the research. Coverage is unfarmable, the reserve grid makes 2,000 words feel finite and conquerable, and the generated reading is a genuine capability no off-the-shelf app has. It also reuses your Leitner scheduler almost unchanged.

Cost: it's austere. There is no world, no characters, no humour — the pleasure is the pleasure of a well-calibrated instrument, which is real but narrow. If your motivation dips, this design has nothing to catch you with except the number. It is also the weakest of the three on the “fun and engaging” requirement, and you should weigh that honestly.

◆
Concept B

In Città — the task world

The app is a small Italian city. Each district is a domain of real things you might need to do — order breakfast, get a prescription, argue with a landlord — and each mission is a task with an outcome you either achieve or don't. Achievement is a shelf of Posso… statements: things you have demonstrably done.

This is task-based language teaching taken literally. TBLT showed an effect of d = 0.93 across 52 long-term classroom programmes, and its mechanism is exactly what a solo app usually lacks: language used to accomplish something, with a result that can fail. It also feeds the ideal-L2-self mechanism harder than any other design here, because every mission is a concrete picture of you handling a situation.

Why this one might be right for you

It is the most fun of the three, and the best answer to “app as the whole course”, because tasks naturally force all four strands: you read the scene, rehearse the language, produce speech under pressure, then review. If you're learning Italian because you want to use it in Italy, this design is organised around that and the other two aren't.

What Concept B gets right, and what it costs

Right: it's the only one of the three where all four strands fall out of the structure automatically, and it's the most enjoyable. Missions give the app a natural unit of work, a natural stopping point, and a natural reason to speak. Districts gating on stage is an elegant way to make the acquisition ladder feel like progress rather than restriction.

Cost: content-hungry. Every mission needs a scene, a model dialogue, a rehearsal set and a working conversational agent — a lot per twelve minutes of study. The coverage engine also becomes less visible here, so the 2,000-word goal risks fading into the background unless the Taccuino screen works hard.

◆
Concept C

La Serie — the serial you can just about read

The app is an ongoing drama set in Bologna, written fresh for you at exactly the level you can handle. You keep going because you want to know what happens next — and the only way to get the next episode is to do the language work that makes it readable.

This concept bets on the strand every app neglects and the research rates highly: massive comprehensible input. Extensive reading returned d = 0.46 on controlled comparisons and d = 0.71 pre–post, across reading, vocabulary, fluency, writing, oral proficiency and motivation. The catch has always been that graded readers are written for an imaginary average learner. Yours would be written for your actual lexicon, sentence by sentence.

Why this one might be right for you

Because narrative is the one motivator that doesn't decay into a chore. The research also supplies an unusual permission here: interleaving hurts connected narrative, so this is the one part of a course allowed to be blocked and immersive. And a serial gives words something no flashcard can — a place they happened.

What Concept C gets right, and what it costs

Right: it is the only design where review is intrinsically motivated. Words come back because the plot needs them, not because a queue says so — which quietly solves the hardest retention problem in consumer language apps. It also delivers the input strand at a volume neither other concept approaches, and narrative is the one context where the research actively endorses not interleaving.

Cost: it's the most technically demanding. Generating 340 words of coherent, plot-advancing Italian constrained to a specific lexicon and a specific grammatical stage, episode after episode, is a real engineering problem. It's also the thinnest on task-based outcomes — you speak as a character, but you never accomplish anything you'd actually need to do in Bologna.

What I'd build

Not one of the three. The comparison table gives away the answer: each concept is strongest at a different strand, and a course needs all four. So the recommendation is B as the shell, C as its input engine, A as its progress surface — which is less of a compromise than it sounds, because it assigns each design to the job it already wins.

B
In Città is the app you open

Task-based was an explicit requirement, and only B is genuinely organised around tasks. The city map is the home screen, missions are the unit of work, and the Posso… shelf is the primary achievement surface. It also carries output and fluency — the two strands the current app has none of.

C
La Serie becomes a district in that city

The serial is how you get input volume, and it solves review motivation better than any queue: the next episode is written to reuse the words that are due. Treat it as an ongoing thread you dip into between missions, not a separate app. Marta's flat is a place on the map.

A
Copertura is the “Io” tab

The reserve grid, the coverage curve and the stage panel are the honest achievement screens, and the mapping cards and articles strand are the language-focused work. This is Concept A demoted from whole app to one tab — which is exactly where an instrument belongs.

The build order

Everything above depends on the engine, so the engine is not phase one — it is phase one, and it's mostly a data problem rather than a UI one.

  1. The lexicon. Load De Mauro's fondamentale list in frequency order with EN and PL glosses. Extend storage.js from two word states to five. This alone unlocks the coverage metric and kills the streak.
  2. Coverage + the reserve grid. A single screen, cheap to build, and it immediately replaces the current dashboard's “% complete” with something meaningful.
  3. The stage model. Infer stage from production accuracy; gate grading rather than content. Retro-fit it to the existing grammar drills so nothing marks you wrong above your stage.
  4. Generation against the lexicon. The 95% rule, applied first to short texts. Get this working before building either the serial or the missions, because both depend on it.
  5. Missions with voice. The biggest build, and the one with the biggest measured effect. Start with three missions in one district to prove the four-phase shape.
  6. The serial. Last, because it's the hardest generation problem and the most content-sensitive. One season of twelve short episodes is enough to test whether it holds you.
What this means for the existing code

The Leitner scheduler in srs.js, the theme system, speech playback and the A1–C1 ladder all survive intact. Dashboard.jsx and StreakChip.jsx are the casualties — the streak goes, and “% complete” is replaced by coverage. The four existing modules don't disappear so much as get re-cast: vocab becomes the reserve, grammar becomes stage-gated interludes, conversations become mission phase 3, and stories become the serial.

One thing to decide before any of it

Generation quality is the load-bearing assumption in two of the three concepts, and it's the one I can't verify from research. Before committing to the serial, it's worth spending an afternoon testing whether a model can actually write 340 words of decent Italian constrained to a 600-word lexicon and to stage 3 grammar. If it can, Concept C is the strongest idea here. If it can't, B and A alone still make a complete course.
