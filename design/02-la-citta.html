Does the serial actually work?

Before designing around it, I tested the assumption I'd flagged as unverified: can a model write 340 words of decent Italian confined to a ~600-word lexicon and to stage-3 grammar? I built the measuring apparatus and ran it, rather than guessing.

The setup: an Italian lexicon built from frequency data and lemmatised; eight test texts; and a checker that scores each text two ways — known-word coverage, and grammar-stage violations. Two independent detectors run over every verb, because neither is trustworthy alone. spaCy's Italian model silently mislabels some futures (it tagged andrò as a past tense), while pure suffix rules over-fire on nouns. Every flag got printed and hand-checked.

The answer

Yes — but only if you generate against the lexicon.

Written freely, my Italian sat at 82% coverage with a grammar violation every 73 words. Written against the word list, the same prose hit 97% coverage with zero violations. One future slipped in at 314 words, and a repair pass removed it in a single edit.

The result that actually decides the design

I then measured the same texts against lexicons from 400 up to 3,000 lemmas. This is the finding that matters, and it wasn't what I expected:

Read that gap carefully, because it inverts the usual assumption. You cannot hand a beginner ordinary Italian and expect it to be comprehensible input — not at 600 words, not at 2,000, barely at 3,000. But text written against their known words is readable almost immediately. Generating against the lexicon isn't an optimisation. It is the entire mechanism that makes early reading possible.

Which settles the design question: the serial goes in. It is not a risky bet — it's the only part of the app that can deliver input volume at a beginner's level at all.

Four honest caveats

1. I wrote the texts. The measurement is objective — a program that doesn't know what I intended — but the generation isn't blind. This shows the capability exists; it isn't a benchmark against other models.

2. The lexicon is a proxy. I built it from subtitle frequency data, which is dialogue-skewed and missing everyday concrete nouns: no porta below 1,500, and no tavolo, letto, sedia or vestito even at 3,000. I had to write around those gaps, which visibly stiffened the prose.

3. That gap is the argument for using the real De Mauro list. His third tier is alta disponibilità — "highly available" words everyone knows but which are rare in corpora. Tavolo. Letto. Sedia. He solved this exact problem in 1980. Use his list, not a frequency dump.

4. Quality drops measurably. Lexical variety fell from a type–token ratio of 0.53 to 0.44 under constraint. The constrained text is correct and readable, but plainer. A human should still read episode one before you ship it.

One design system

You asked for consistency and for something funky. Those pull in the same direction if the system is small enough to never break. This one is four rules and six colours, applied to every single surface in the app.

The four rules
  1. 3px ink border on everything. Cards, buttons, pills, the phone itself. Nothing floats without an outline.
  2. Hard offset shadow, never blurred. A flat 3–4px block of ink, down-right. It's what makes the whole thing feel like stickers rather than glass.
  3. 18px radius, flat fills. No gradients anywhere. Colour is always a single confident block.
  4. Colour carries meaning, not decoration. Each district owns a hue and keeps it everywhere it appears — on the map, in its scenes, on its badge.
Il Bar · #FF4D3D Il Mercato · #FFD23F La Stazione · #2BB3FF La Farmacia · #00D9A3 L'Officina · #6B2FE8 Il Cinema · #FF7BC8

Type is Futura for anything that shouts — headings, numbers, the big coverage figure — because it's geometric, Italian-modern and reads as confident rather than cute. Body copy is a neutral UI sans, and every number that needs comparing is monospaced so digits line up.

One thing this style costs you

Thick borders and hard shadows eat space, which is fine on a phone but punishing for long reading. So the serial's reading view drops the system on purpose: no borders, no shadows, a serif face, generous leading. It's the one screen allowed to be calm, and the contrast makes it feel like a different, quieter place — which is exactly what you want when someone settles in to read.

How every coverage figure on this page is computed

These numbers now come out of the app's own rankWeight() (src/shared/coverage.js), not out of my head. A word contributes 1/rank — Zipf — normalised so the whole 2,000 comes to 86% of running text. Two assumptions carry the rest: the learner acquires words roughly in frequency order, which is what La Riserva is built to make happen; and coverage counts a word once it is known or solid, not merely met.

That last point is why screen 01 says 715 parole where the Riserva says 834. The reservoir holds 834 words the learner has touched — 487 solid, 228 known, 119 still in progress — but only the 715 at known-or-better are claimed as coverage. The count beside the percentage is the count the percentage is made of.

The uncomfortable consequence, stated rather than hidden: on day one, 62 words in frequency order is already 49,6%. That is arithmetically correct — function words dominate tokens, and the top 100 words really are about half of everything written — but it is a poor description of what a beginner can do. It also makes the six-month curve on screen 19 look flat, because Zipf front-loads all the movement. Whether Giorno 1 should show this quantity at all is an open design question.

Not the same number as the sweep below. The table in the Cinema section ("600 → 97,2%") measures coverage of text written against the learner's lexicon. This figure measures coverage of ordinary running Italian. A 600-word learner is at 73,3% of ordinary Italian and 97,2% of prose written for them — and that gap is the entire argument for generating against the lexicon.

Il Cinema — La Serie

The district the experiment earned its place. A Bologna drama written fresh at your exact level, where the next episode is generated to reuse the words that are due for review — so spaced repetition stops being a queue and becomes the plot.

The Italian in screen 14 is not invented for the mockup. It's the opening of B_constr_1.txt from the experiment, which measured 99.6% coverage against the 600-word lexicon with zero grammar-stage violations.

When it opens — and why that number

Il Cinema is the last district to unlock, and the threshold isn't a pacing guess. It comes straight out of the sweep in section 01.

Coverage of constrained Italian, by how many words the learner knows
Words knownBest achievable coverageReadable?
40090.1%No — roughly 1 unknown word in 10. That's decoding, not reading.
60097.2%Yes — comfortably past Nation's 95% floor.
1 00098.5%Yes, with room to introduce new words on purpose.

Below about 600 known words there simply isn't enough vocabulary to build a story out of. The generator would either write nonsense or lean on words you don't have — and a story you have to decode word-by-word kills the one thing the serial is for, which is wanting to know what happens next.

The unlock condition

600 solid words — not merely met, but in the top Leitner box, which you reach by recalling a word correctly after a week away — plus two districts finished. The word count makes the story possible; the districts make sure you've spoken Italian out loud before you're asked to read a page of it.

That's roughly 25–35 hours in. Until then Il Cinema shows on the map with a padlock and a live counter — mancano 538 parole — because a visible locked door you're walking toward beats a door you didn't know was there.

What to build first

The experiment changed the order. Generation is no longer the risky thing you defer to the end — it's proven, and it's what makes the reading district possible at all. But it depends entirely on the lexicon, so that still comes first.

Phase 1 · the lexicon

Get De Mauro's real vocabolario di base in, not a frequency dump — the experiment showed exactly why. Extend storage.js from two word states to five (unseen / met / learning / known / solid). This alone gives you the coverage number and kills the streak.

Ship after this: screen 01 and screen 10. The map can have one district. You'd already have a better home screen than the app has now.

Phase 2 · the stage model

Infer stage from production accuracy, and gate grading rather than content. Retro-fit it to the existing grammar drills so nothing marks you wrong above your stage. Screens 12 and 20 fall out of this.

Phase 3 · L'Officina

Mapping cards, word detail, the Riserva grid, the articles strand. Mostly a re-skin of the vocab module you already have, plus the Polish-route content — which is writing, not engineering. Cheapest district per unit of value.

Phase 4 · Il Cinema

Now safe to build. Wire the checker from research/gen-experiment/ into the pipeline as a gate: generate, measure coverage and stage violations, repair or regenerate, then release. The experiment showed a single repair edit was enough at 314 words — but keep the gate, because that's the only reason the 95% promise holds.

Phase 5 · scenes with voice

The biggest build and the biggest measured effect. Start with three scenes in Il Mercato to prove the four-phase shape before making more districts.

What survives from the current app

More than you'd think. The Leitner scheduler in srs.js, the theme system, speech playback, and the A1–C1 ladder all carry over. The four existing modules get re-cast rather than deleted: vocab → La Riserva, grammar → stage-gated cards, conversations → scene phase 3, stories → Il Cinema.

The casualties are StreakChip.jsx and the current Dashboard.jsx headline: “% complete” measures content consumed, which is the activity metric the research says to stop optimising.

The one thing I'd still check by hand

Read episode one yourself before trusting the generator at volume. The experiment showed constrained Italian is correct, but lexical variety dropped measurably (TTR 0.53 → 0.44). Correct and dull is a real failure mode for something whose whole job is to make you want the next episode.
