Evidence review

What actually teaches a language

Sixty years of second-language research, the cognitive-psychology findings that underpin every flashcard app ever built, and the awkward evidence about what those apps really deliver — assembled into guidelines you can build against. With a long detour into Italian, and into what changes when the learner already speaks English, or Polish.

How memory actually works

The most robust findings in this entire report come not from language teaching but from cognitive psychology. They are old, replicated to death, and still routinely ignored.

Here is the single most uncomfortable fact in learning science, and it comes from a 2008 experiment in Science that has been replicated relentlessly ever since. Students learned Swahili–English word pairs. Some kept restudying words they had already got right. Others kept being tested on words they had already got right. A week later, the restudy group remembered about 36% of the list. The repeated-testing group remembered about 80%.1

Extra studying after you first get something right does essentially nothing. Extra retrieving does almost everything. And the cruellest part: when the students predicted their own scores, their predictions were uncorrelated with reality. Restudying feels like learning. It is a feeling, not an effect.12

80%Recalled after repeated testing
36%Recalled after repeated study
r ≈ 0Learners' ability to tell the difference
Design consequence

Every minute a learner spends looking at a word list is a minute stolen from a minute spent trying to produce the word from an empty screen. A "review" screen that shows the answer is not review. Make the default interaction recall, and reserve passive presentation for genuinely new material.

The spacing effect, and the number nobody uses

That retrieval should be spread out is common knowledge. What is not common knowledge is that there is an optimal gap, and that it depends entirely on how long you want the memory to last. Cepeda and colleagues taught over 1,350 people a set of facts, then reviewed them after gaps of up to 3.5 months, then tested them up to a year later.3

The result is a ridge, not a cliff. Widening the gap helps — up to a point — then hurts. And the peak moves: the optimal gap is roughly 10–20% of how long you want to remember something. Want it for a week? Review after a day. Want it for a year? Review after about a month. Most apps review on a schedule tuned to feel busy, which is to say tuned for a retention interval of about a week.3

What the meta-analysis says

Kim and Webb pooled 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N = 3,411) specifically on second-language spaced practice. Spacing beats massing with a medium-to-large effect. Two findings matter for design:4

  • On an immediate test, short and long gaps look identical. On a delayed test, long gaps win clearly. If you A/B-test your scheduler against next-day performance, you will select for the worse algorithm.
  • Expanding intervals are not reliably better than equal intervals. The elaborate expanding schedule inherited from SuperMemo is not, on the evidence, the load-bearing part. The load-bearing part is that the gaps exist at all and are long enough.
Half-life regressionSettles & Meeder, ACL 2016

Rather than hand-tuning intervals, model each item's memory half-life as a learned function of practice history and lexical features, fitted on real recall data. Trained on 13 million Duolingo learning traces, it cut recall-prediction error by over 45% against standard baselines and lifted daily engagement by 12% in a live study.

Open-source implementation and dataset published.

Two caveats worth knowing before you copy it. Half-life regression is fitted on in-app recall, so it optimises for recall of app items, not language ability. And later work on adaptive forgetting curves and on optimal-control formulations of review scheduling has shown the problem is genuinely open — there is no settled best algorithm.3940

Desirable difficulties: when making it harder makes it better

Robert and Elizabeth Bjork named the paradox in 1994: conditions that slow you down during practice often speed up long-term retention and transfer. Spacing is one. Retrieval is another. Interleaving — mixing item types instead of blocking them into same-kind drills — is a third.637

Blocked practice feels far more productive, which is precisely why it survives. In one classic demonstration, learners who interleaved got 63% correct on a delayed test against 20% for the blocked group — despite the blocked group performing better during practice.6

But not always

Interleaving is not universally good. It hurts when the material is a connected narrative or a system that needs to be understood as a whole, because switching interferes with building the coherent representation.38 The practical rule: block during first exposure to a new system, interleave everything thereafter. Teach the Italian passato prossimo as a coherent thing once; then never again let a learner do twenty passato-prossimo items in a row.

What has to be in the box

Memory science tells you how to schedule practice. It says nothing about what to practise. For that you need second-language acquisition research, which is messier, more contested, and — on a few points — unanimous.

The cleanest organising idea in the field is Paul Nation's four strands. Every activity in a language course falls into one of four categories, and a well-built course gives each roughly a quarter of the total time.7

Nation's four strands, and how a typical vocabulary app scores
StrandWhat it isTypical app
Meaning-focused input Reading and listening for the message: stories, video, conversation as listener partial — usually sentence-level only
Meaning-focused output Speaking and writing to convey something real to someone absent — translation is not communication
Language-focused learning Deliberate study of vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, spelling strong — this is what apps are good at
Fluency development Working with already-known material under time pressure to get fast rare — timers exist but material is always new

Read that table as a product roadmap. The typical app is excellent at one strand out of four and structurally incapable of a second. That is not a bug you can patch with better flashcards; it is the shape of the category.

The vocabulary numbers

How many words does a learner actually need? Nation's lexical-profiling work gives startlingly specific answers, and they are bigger than app marketing implies.845

Target95% coverage98% coverage
Unscripted spoken English3,0006,000–7,000
Written text / novels4,000–5,0008,000–9,000

Why 98%? Because that is roughly the threshold for unassisted comprehension — about one unknown word in fifty. At 95% (one in twenty) comprehension is possible but effortful and support is needed. Nation's condition for the meaning-focused input strand to exist at all is that 95–98% of running words are already known.78

Design consequence

An app that shows learners authentic content before they have the vocabulary to hit 95% coverage is not giving them input; it is giving them noise. Either grade the text to the learner's known-word set, or gloss aggressively so the effective coverage clears the threshold. Knowing exactly which words a learner knows is the single biggest structural advantage a digital course has over a book — and most apps waste it.

Input, output, interaction

Krashen's claim that comprehensible input is sufficient for acquisition has not survived. The consensus after decades of argument is that input is necessary but not enough: Swain's output hypothesis (producing language forces you to notice gaps and test hypotheses in a way comprehension never does) and Long's interaction hypothesis (negotiating meaning when communication breaks down drives acquisition) fill the remainder.910

Extensive reading — large quantities of self-selected, easy text — has a meta-analytic effect of d = 0.46 for controlled comparisons and d = 0.71 pre–post, across 34 studies and 3,942 learners. Effects held across reading comprehension, vocabulary, fluency, writing, oral proficiency and motivation. Notably, effects were larger when text choice was constrained and some accountability existed — a pure free-for-all is not optimal.1136

Explicit grammar: the argument that was actually settled

This is the most politically charged question in language teaching, and the meta-analytic answer has been stable for twenty-five years.

Norris & Ortega (2000)49 studies

Focused instruction produces large target-oriented gains. Explicit instruction outperforms implicit instruction. Focus-on-Form (attention to form inside a communicative activity) and Focus-on-FormS (systematic form teaching) produce equivalent and large effects. Instructional effects are durable.

Language Learning 50(3), 417–528.
Spada & Tomita (2010)41 studies

Explicit beats implicit for both simple and complex grammatical features — and explicit instruction contributes to spontaneous use, not merely to controlled test performance.

Language Learning 60(2), 263–308.
The honest objection

Critics — including Spada herself in later work — point out that the outcome measures in these studies mostly tap explicit knowledge. If you test with a grammar exercise, explicit teaching will win. Whether it produces the implicit knowledge that drives real-time speech is less certain.41 The defensible position: explicit explanation is a cheap, high-yield accelerator, but it is not a substitute for the hours of use that build automaticity.

Corrective feedback

Li's meta-analysis of 33 studies found a medium overall effect for corrective feedback that was maintained over time. The subtle finding: explicit feedback produced larger immediate effects, but implicit feedback was better maintained.12 Elsewhere, prompts (which push the learner to self-correct) outperform recasts (which simply supply the right form) — consistent with everything the retrieval literature predicts.

Design consequence

The standard app pattern — wrong answer, instant red X, correct answer displayed — is the least effective feedback shape available. It supplies the form instead of prompting retrieval. A better sequence: flag that something is wrong, indicate where, let the learner try again, and only then reveal. You are converting a presentation event into a second retrieval attempt.

Tasks, and the teachability constraint

Task-based language teaching — organising a course around authentic things to accomplish rather than a grammar sequence — showed an overall effect of d = 0.93 across 52 studies of long-term classroom programmes.13 That is a large effect for a whole-programme intervention, though the studies vary in rigour and the comparison conditions are heterogeneous.42

Against that sits Pienemann's teachability hypothesis: learners acquire certain structures in a fixed developmental order, and instruction only helps when the learner is one step away from the target stage. Teaching a structure far above a learner's current stage produces memorised forms, not acquisition.14 This has been validated specifically for Italian.27 We will come back to what that order looks like in Italian — it is unusually well documented.

03

What apps actually achieve

The efficacy literature is real, growing, and considerably more nuanced than either the marketing or the backlash suggests.

Start with the broad picture. Sung, Chang and Yang pooled 44 studies spanning 1993–2013 with 9,154 participants and found mobile-assisted language learning produced a mean effect of d = 0.55 over comparison conditions — a solid moderate effect.15 Later syntheses using mobile apps specifically have reported larger figures, and a 2022 meta-analysis found vocabulary effects reaching d = 1.28 when treatment ran ten weeks or longer.16

Read effect sizes with the comparison in mind

That same 2022 meta-analysis flagged high risk of bias and low quality of evidence across all included studies.16 And an effect size is always relative to something. "App beats no instruction" is nearly meaningless. "App beats a well-taught class" is the claim that matters, and it is much rarer.

Speech: the fastest-moving area

Automatic speech recognition for pronunciation training has a meta-analytic effect of g = 0.69 across 15 studies. The moderators are instructive: ASR works well for segmental pronunciation (individual sounds) but only weakly for suprasegmental features (stress, rhythm, intonation), and it works far better when paired with explicit corrective feedback than when it just scores you.2147

In the same analysis, chatbots scored g = 0.841 against traditional instruction, g = 0.645 against talking to a peer — but only g = 0.323 against other non-conversational technology (apps, websites, learning platforms).22

In other words: most of the measured advantage of an AI tutor is the advantage of using something interactive at all. Against a decent existing app, the marginal gain is small. Build the conversational layer — the evidence supports it — but do not expect it to be the thing that makes or breaks the product.

The consistent finding across every study

Look across the whole efficacy literature and one asymmetry keeps recurring: apps develop receptive skills faster than productive ones, and written skills faster than oral ones. Loewen's Turkish learners showed it.18 Even Smith's positive Spanish results came with learners themselves reporting the lack of practical listening, speaking, pronunciation and conversational support — despite having made real gains in exactly those areas.20 Research on Duolingo's implicit pronunciation teaching found the same gap.43

That learner perception is worth taking seriously even where the data contradicts it. People who do not feel they can speak will not go and speak, whatever the test says.

Streaks, and the motivation trap

Every hour of instruction is worthless if nobody shows up. But the mechanisms that make people show up can quietly select against the behaviours that produce learning.

The dominant framework is self-determination theory: motivation is durable when three needs are met — autonomy (I chose this), competence (I'm getting better), and relatedness (I'm doing this with and for people). Studies of gamified language apps consistently find that badges, streaks and leaderboards do raise engagement, and that they do so specifically by feeding those three needs.2324

The complementary framework is Dörnyei's L2 Motivational Self System, where motivation flows from the gap between who you are now and a vivid ideal L2 self — a concrete mental image of yourself using the language. Motivation strength tracks the vividness of that image, and interventions that deliberately build it have been shown to work.25

Where streaks go wrong

A streak is a controlled motivator wearing an autonomy costume. It rewards showing up, not learning, so a rational learner optimises for the cheapest qualifying action — which is exactly what Jiang et al. observed: 20–22% of participants did more than half their lessons at the lowest available difficulty, and total hours logged had a near-zero, non-significant correlation with actual proficiency.19

Time-in-app is not the outcome. If your metric is time-in-app, you will build a product that maximises the thing that does not correlate with learning.

What to do instead

The self-regulated-learning literature offers a strong alternative lever. A meta-analysis of SRL interventions in L2 contexts found effects of g = 1.39 on learning achievement, 0.45 on self-efficacy, 0.40 on strategy use — with metacognitively-grounded interventions outperforming purely cognitive ones.26

Concretely: teaching learners to set their own goals, monitor their progress and reflect on what is and is not working produces larger achievement gains than most instructional interventions in this report. That is autonomy and competence in SDT terms, and it is largely absent from consumer language apps, which tend to make every scheduling decision for the learner.

Motivation design that isn't a streak
  • Show mastery, not activity. "You can now understand 71% of a typical Italian news article" beats "14-day streak" because it feeds competence with something true.
  • Let learners choose. Genuine choice over topic, order and difficulty serves autonomy. Choice among three identical lessons does not.
  • Make the ideal self concrete. Ask, at onboarding, what specific situation they want to handle — and then show progress against that situation.
  • Protect the streak from the learner. If you keep streaks, make the qualifying action a real retrieval session, and let people bank rest days without loss.
  • Don't neglect relatedness. It is the SDT need solo apps serve worst. Collaborative mobile-assisted learning is a substantial research area in its own right,50 and a leaderboard is a very thin substitute for learning alongside someone.

05

Sidekick or syllabus?

The most consequential product decision: is this app the whole course, or a component of one? The evidence supports both — but they are genuinely different products.

Model A

App as support

The app handles language-focused learning — vocabulary, morphology, spelling, listening discrimination — and something else handles input, output and interaction.

  • Plays to the strength. Deliberate, spaced, retrieval-based practice is exactly what software does better than a classroom, where a teacher cannot give individual feedback at scale.21
  • Frees the human hours. Blended-learning research finds outcomes improve when a substantial share of contact time is displaced online — because the remaining face-to-face time gets spent on communication rather than drills.
  • Lower burden of proof. You need to be better than the learner's existing homework, not better than a curriculum.

Build: a superb scheduler, aggressive personalisation to a known-word model, tight loops, export and integration. Skip: culture modules, chat, syllabus framing.

Model B

App as the whole course

The app is the learner's only structured contact with Italian. This is what most consumer apps implicitly claim and what the efficacy studies actually test.

  • It demonstrably gets people somewhere. ~112 hours to fourth-semester receptive proficiency is a real result.19
  • The four strands become your problem. You now owe the learner meaning-focused input, output, and fluency development — not just language-focused learning.7
  • Ceiling risk is real. Every study shows the productive and oral gap. A course that never requires speech will not produce speakers.

Build: graded extensive reading and listening, a conversational agent with voice input,22 real tasks with outcomes, and explicit grammar sequenced to the acquisition order.

What makes an effective programme, either way

Strip out the delivery mechanism and the ingredients of an effective language programme are remarkably consistent across the literature:

  1. A balance across the four strands, with roughly equal time to each.7
  2. Massive comprehensible input at 95–98% known-word coverage, with enough volume for repetition to do its work.811
  3. Required output, because noticing the gap between what you mean and what you can say is what drives restructuring.10
  4. Explicit form-focus, either embedded in communication or systematic — both work equally well.9
  5. Feedback that prompts self-correction rather than supplying the answer.12
  6. Distributed, retrieval-based review with gaps sized to the target retention interval.134
  7. A sequence that respects developmental readiness rather than textbook convenience.14
  8. Metacognitive training, so learners can steer their own practice.26
  9. Real tasks with real outcomes, not exercises.13
  10. Motivation grounded in a concrete future self, not in loss aversion.25
The CEFR is your friend here

The Council of Europe's CEFR Companion Volume gives calibrated can-do descriptors across reading, listening, speaking, writing, and — newer — mediation and plurilingual competence.34 Two uses: it converts vague progress into concrete "I can" statements that feed the ideal-self mechanism, and it gives you an externally-validated skeleton for a syllabus, which is otherwise very hard to design credibly from scratch.

06

Italian, specifically

Italian is unusually well served by acquisition research — there is a thirty-year Italian-language research programme with data most languages would envy. Use it.

Two structural facts shape everything about teaching Italian. The first is that its orthography is transparent: spelling maps to sound with near-total consistency. In Seymour's fourteen-country comparison of first-year reading, children learning Italian, Spanish, German, Greek and Finnish were at ceiling on word reading by the end of grade one. English children were at 34%.28

For your product this means: decoding is essentially free. An Italian learner who knows the handful of rules (c/g before front vowels, gl, gn, sc, double consonants, stress) can pronounce any written word and spell any heard word. Do not spend the curriculum budget an English course would spend on spelling. Spend it on the double consonants and on stress, which are the two places the mapping actually costs something — and where the phonemic contrast is real (cade / cadde, papa / papà).

The second fact is that Italian's core vocabulary is extraordinarily well characterised. Tullio De Mauro's vocabolario di base identifies about 7,500 words in three tiers, of which the innermost lessico fondamentale — roughly 2,000 words — accounts for around 86% of everything spoken and written.30

A concrete curriculum spine

De Mauro's tiers give you a ready-made, empirically grounded vocabulary sequence that no amount of corpus work would improve on cheaply. Teach the fondamentale 2,000 first to buy 86% coverage, then the alto uso and alta disponibilità tiers out to 7,500 — which lands the learner in the vicinity of the coverage thresholds from section 02. Sequence within tiers by frequency; that is where a scheduler earns its keep.

The acquisition sequences — the most useful table in this report

The Progetto di Pavia, running since the mid-1980s, tracked 20 learners of Italian aged 12–48 across eight different first languages (Albanian, Arabic, Chichewa, Chinese, English, Mooré, German, Tigrinya) through roughly 120 hours of recorded speech.27 From that corpus come implicational sequences: orders in which structures reliably emerge, regardless of first language. Learners move through at different speeds, but not in different orders.

What this means for your syllabus

Teach in the order structures actually emerge, and do not grade a learner on a structure that sits beyond their current stage. That does not mean hiding the congiuntivo — it should absolutely be in the input, because exposure is what eventually makes it learnable. It means not requiring correct production of it, and not treating participle agreement errors as failures in a learner who is still stabilising the article.27

Chini's summary is blunt about the alternative: forcing paradigms too early produces memorised forms with no system to attach to.

The same research programme describes three broad learner varieties every learner passes through — pre-basic (no grammatical categories, purely pragmatic ordering, extreme context dependence), basic (predicate-and-argument structure, bare verb forms, semantic ordering), and post-basic (inflected nouns and verbs, syntactic ordering).27 Fine-grained morphology teaching only pays off once a learner is entering the post-basic variety. Before that, the useful investment is rich, comprehensible, motivating input plus useful formulaic chunks.

Grammatical gender: the trainable part

Italian gender is not arbitrary, and this is a design opportunity. Word-final phonemes are reliable cues, and learners are demonstrably sensitive to them — studies of L2 Italian show a clear implicational order of difficulty across noun endings. The catch: roughly a third of both masculine and feminine "ambiguous" nouns carry no gender marking at all, which is precisely where learners fail.29

Design consequence

Split your gender curriculum in two. Transparent nouns (-o / -a, and the reliable suffixes like -zione, -tà, -ore) should be taught as a rule and drilled to automaticity — that is cheap coverage. Opaque nouns (-e endings and irregulars) must be learned item-by-item, which means they belong in the spaced-repetition system with their article attached. Never store a noun without its article; store il ponte, never ponte.

How much Italian, and how long

The US Foreign Service Institute classes Italian as a Category I language — among the easiest for English speakers — at roughly 600–750 class hours to reach professional working proficiency (about upper B2 / low C1), under intensive full-time instruction with native teachers.35

Set that against the app numbers from section 03 and the picture is clear and worth being honest with users about: 27 hours buys a real, measurable sublevel. 112 hours buys fourth-semester receptive ability. Professional fluency is an order of magnitude beyond either, and no app is going to shortcut it.

If your learner speaks English

A large free head start on vocabulary, and four specific, predictable places where English actively gets in the way.

The head start is real and measurable. The cognate facilitation effect is one of the most robust findings in bilingual psycholinguistics: words that are similar in form and meaning across two languages are recognised and produced faster and more accurately than non-cognates, in both languages, in speech and in print.31 Vocabulary growth is measurably boosted by the number of cognates a language pair shares, and the effect extends beyond words to easing syntactic processing.

English and Italian share thousands, through Latin directly and through Norman French. Nazione, università, possibile, condizione, difficoltà require essentially no learning. And the mappings are systematic: English -tion → Italian -zione; -ty → -tà; -ous → -oso; -ly → -mente.

Design consequence

Teach the mappings, not the words. A learner who internalises five suffix correspondences unlocks thousands of items at once — a far better return than any individual flashcard. Then use the spaced-repetition budget you just freed on the words that are genuinely opaque.

And explicitly inoculate against false friends, which are the direct cost of this strategy: attualmente (currently, not actually), eventualmente (possibly, not eventually), libreria (bookshop, not library), parenti (relatives, not parents), morbido (soft, not morbid), fattoria (farm, not factory).

The four predictable difficulties

  1. Grammatical gender. English has none, so there is no L1 hook whatsoever and every noun carries an extra bit of information the learner is not used to storing. See the transparent/opaque split above.29
  2. The perfective/imperfective contrast. English marks aspect inconsistently and lexically, so the passato prossimo / imperfetto distinction has no clean L1 anchor. English speakers typically over-generalise the passato prossimo and under-use the imperfetto for background and description.
  3. The congiuntivo. Last in the acquisition sequence for a reason, and English's residual subjunctive ("if I were") is too vestigial to help. Expect this late, teach it late, and do not gate progress on it.27
  4. Clitic pronouns and pro-drop. Glielo, ce n'è, me lo dai — clitic clusters have no English analogue, and English's obligatory subject pronoun leads learners to produce io penso che... where a native speaker would just say penso che...

Add pronunciation. English speakers reliably struggle with Italian's pure vowels (English diphthongises almost every long vowel), with geminate consonants (nono vs nonno — a real phonemic contrast that English lacks entirely), and with the trilled r. Since ASR is strongest exactly on segmental features, this is where speech technology pays off best.21

If your learner speaks Polish

A very different profile: significant hidden advantages that no course tells them about, one notorious difficulty, and a research finding that should change how you write explanations.

Start with the finding, because it is the most practically important thing in this section and it is counter-intuitive. Toth, Hlava and Gómez-Pablos studied Slavic-speaking university learners (L1 Slovak, L2 English) acquiring Italian and Spanish as third languages, and included a metalinguistic task asking them to explain aspectual contrasts.32

93%Compared Romance forms to their Slavic L1
5%Compared to English, their L2
2%Compared to another Romance language

These learners had studied English for roughly twelve years. Many were studying more than one Romance language. And when reasoning about how Italian tense and aspect work, they reached for Slovak in 161 of 173 cases. Moreover, the sentence pairs that could be translated directly into Slovak elicited noticeably more accurate reasoning.32

Design consequence

If your app teaches Italian through English to a Polish speaker, your grammatical explanations are being silently re-translated into Polish before they land — and where Polish offers no parallel, the explanation degrades. An Italian course for Polish speakers should anchor explanations in Polish categories, not English ones, even if the interface language is English.

This is a genuine competitive gap. Almost every commercial Italian course is built for an English-speaking mind, and the research says that is the wrong mental scaffolding for a Slavic-speaking learner.

The one big difficulty: articles

Polish has no articles. Neither does any other Slavic language except Bulgarian and Macedonian. And the research is consistent that this is not a beginner problem that resolves — article errors persist into advanced proficiency. Studies of Polish learners acquiring English articles document exactly this, and work on Polish-L1 learners acquiring Spanish as a third language found articles remained one of the largest error sources even among the most advanced learners.33

Italian makes this harder than English does, because Italian articles are not just definite/indefinite — they inflect for gender and number and change shape phonologically (il, lo, l', i, gli, la, le), and they fuse with prepositions (del, nella, sugli). And Italian uses the definite article in places English does not: with possessives (la mia casa), with generics (il caffè fa bene), with countries and languages.

Design consequence

For Slavic-L1 learners, articles deserve a dedicated, long-running, high-frequency strand of their own, running the entire length of the course — not a lesson in week two. Treat it the way a well-designed course treats tones for a Mandarin learner: as a permanent thread. Note also that the acquisition sequence puts definite before indefinite articles,27 so sequence accordingly.

The aspect advantage, and its trap

Polish aspect is a genuine asset — but the mapping to Italian is not one-to-one, and the Toth study documents exactly where it breaks. In Slavic languages aspect is lexico-grammatical: it is baked into each verb's lexical identity, with perfective and imperfective forming lexical pairs. In Italian it is expressed through tense morphology in the past.32

Where a Slavic pair exists, transfer is clean and the learner is far ahead. Where it does not, the intuition fails. The study's own example: Slovak byť ("to be") is imperfective and has no perfective counterpart — so both Era una bella giornata and È stata una bella giornata collapse into the same Slovak sentence, and the Italian contrast becomes invisible.32

The broader theoretical point matters too. Third-language acquisition research has moved away from wholesale transfer models toward property-by-property accounts — the Linguistic Proximity and Scalpel models — where transfer happens feature by feature from whichever prior language offers a match.3246 Practically: a Polish speaker will transfer aspect from Polish, may transfer some vocabulary through English (Latinate cognates work through both routes), and gets no help from either on articles.

What a Polish-first Italian course would look like
  • Articles as a permanent strand from day one, sequenced definite → indefinite → fused prepositions, with heavy spaced retrieval.
  • Explain passato prossimo / imperfetto via Polish aspect pairs — then explicitly flag the verbs where the pair doesn't exist and the intuition will mislead.
  • Skip most of the pronunciation curriculum an English-speaker course needs. Spend that budget on geminates and on stress placement, which Polish (fixed penultimate stress) does not prepare a learner for.
  • Lean hard on cognates, but route them through both Polish Latinate vocabulary (rewolucja / rivoluzione, uniwersytet / università) and English.
  • Tell the learner about their advantages. Knowing you have a structural head start on gender, aspect, conjugation and pronunciation is itself a motivational asset in ideal-self terms.25

Eighteen guidelines

Everything above, compressed into things you can actually build. Ordered by how much difference they make relative to how much they cost.

Make retrieval the default interaction

If a screen shows the answer before the learner has tried to produce it, it is not practice. Restudying feels like learning and isn't.1

Size review gaps to the retention target, not to engagement

Optimal gap ≈ 10–20% of how long you want the memory to last. And never validate a scheduler on next-day performance — short and long gaps look identical immediately and diverge only later.34

Model memory strength per item, don't hand-tune intervals

Half-life regression cut prediction error 45%+ and lifted engagement 12%. But fit it on outcomes you actually care about, not just in-app recall.5

Block on first exposure, interleave forever after

New systems need coherent presentation once. After that, mixing item types beats blocking them, even though it feels worse.638

Track a known-word model and grade all content against it

Input only works above ~95% coverage. Knowing precisely which words a learner knows is software's biggest structural advantage over a textbook.78

Audit your product against the four strands

Most apps do one of four well. Name which strands you are not serving, and decide deliberately whether to build them or to be honest that you are a supplement.7

Prompt self-correction before revealing the answer

Wrong → red X → correct answer is the weakest feedback shape. Flag, localise, allow a retry, then reveal. It converts a presentation into a retrieval.12

Teach grammar explicitly — and sequence it developmentally

Explicit instruction wins across meta-analyses. But teach in acquisition order, and don't assess structures beyond the learner's current stage.91014

Require output, not just recognition

Every efficacy study shows the same receptive/productive gap. Multiple choice is not production, and translation is not communication.1018

Build the conversational layer with voice, on mobile

Mobile availability (g 0.79 vs 0.19), voice input (0.81 vs 0.43) and generative AI (0.83 vs 0.47) are the significant moderators. Purpose-built vs generic is not.22

Use ASR for sounds, not for prosody — and pair it with explicit feedback

ASR has a large effect on segmentals, small on suprasegmentals, and works much better when it explains than when it merely scores.21

Replace activity metrics with mastery metrics

Hours logged had a near-zero correlation with proficiency, and a fifth of learners farmed the easiest available lessons. Measure what you want to cause.19

Teach learners to steer their own learning

Self-regulated-learning interventions produced g = 1.39 on achievement — larger than most instructional interventions here, and almost absent from consumer apps.26

Anchor motivation in a concrete future self

Ask what specific situation the learner wants to handle, then report progress against that. CEFR can-do descriptors give you calibrated language for it.2534

For Italian: sequence vocabulary on the vocabolario di base

2,000 words for ~86% coverage, 7,500 for the useful ceiling. An empirically grounded spine you get for free.30

For Italian: split gender teaching by transparency

Rule-teach the transparent endings; item-learn the opaque ones in spaced repetition, always stored with the article attached.29

For English speakers: teach suffix mappings, then inoculate against false friends

Five correspondences unlock thousands of words. Then spend the saved budget on gender, aspect, clitics and geminates — the four predictable failure points.31

For Polish speakers: explain in Polish categories, and make articles permanent

Slavic learners reason about Romance grammar via their L1 93% of the time, after a decade of English. And articles never stop being hard — give them a strand, not a lesson.3233

If you only do three things

One: make every practice interaction a retrieval attempt, with gaps sized to a long retention interval rather than to daily engagement. Two: pick which of Nation's four strands you serve, and say so honestly rather than implying you are a full course. Three: build for the learner's actual first language — for Italian that means the acquisition sequences from Pavia, and for a Polish speaker it means an entirely different course from the one an English speaker needs.
