## Jiang, Rollinson, Plonsky, Gustafson & Pajak (2021) — Evaluating the reading and listening outcomes of beginning-level Duolingo courses
*Foreign Language Annals, 54(4), 974–1002.* DOI: [10.1111/flan.12600](https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12600)

- n = 225 U.S. adults (135 Spanish, 90 French), ages 18–83 (M = 44), minimal prior knowledge,
  Duolingo-only learners who completed Section 5 (beginning content, ~A2).
- Instruments: ACTFL Listening (LPT) and Reading (RPT) Proficiency Tests, remote proctored.
- Results: Spanish — reading M = 4.30/10 (Intermediate Low), listening M = 2.80 (approaching
  Novice High). French — reading M = 4.82 (approaching Intermediate Mid), listening M = 3.61
  (Novice High).
- Median total study time: 112 hours (Spanish 125 h, French 99 h).
- Versus 4th-semester university students (774 Spanish, ~423 French): no significant difference
  on Spanish reading (d = 0.04), Spanish listening (d = −0.24), French listening (d = 0.21);
  Duolingo learners significantly HIGHER on French reading (d = 0.72).
- Correlation between total hours and scores: very small, nonsignificant.
- Limitations (authors' own): only receptive skills tested — no speaking or writing; sample
  older/more educated than university comparison; cross-sectional; self-reported exclusivity;
  partly Duolingo-internal research team.

## Kim & Webb (2022) — The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis
*Language Learning, 72(1), 269–319.* DOI: [10.1111/lang.12479](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479)

- 98 effect sizes, 48 experiments, N = 3,411.
- Spacing has a medium-to-large advantage over massed practice for L2 learning.
- On immediate posttests, short and long gaps are equivalent; on DELAYED posttests, longer
  spacing intervals significantly outperform shorter ones.
- Equal vs expanding spacing schedules: statistically comparable (no reliable advantage for
  expanding schedules).
- Moderators: learning target, number of sessions, practice format, activity, feedback timing,
  retention interval.

## Li (2010) — The Effectiveness of Corrective Feedback in SLA: A Meta-Analysis
*Language Learning, 60(2), 309–365.* DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x)

Abstract (captured from publisher page): 33 primary studies. Findings: (a) medium overall
effect for corrective feedback, maintained over time; (b) the effect of implicit feedback was
better MAINTAINED than that of explicit feedback; (c) published studies did not show larger
effects than dissertations; (d) lab-based studies larger than classroom; (e) shorter treatments
larger than longer; (f) foreign-language contexts larger than second-language contexts.

## Spada & Tomita (2010) — Interactions Between Type of Instruction and Type of Language Feature: A Meta-Analysis
*Language Learning, 60(2), 263–308.* DOI: [10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x)

Abstract (captured from publisher page): 41 studies; features categorized simple/complex
(Hulstijn & de Graaff, 1994); treatments explicit/implicit (Norris & Ortega, 2000). Results:
larger effect sizes for explicit over implicit instruction for BOTH simple and complex
features; explicit instruction contributes to both controlled knowledge and spontaneous use.

## Nakanishi (2015) — A Meta-Analysis of Extensive Reading Research
*TESOL Quarterly, 49(1), 6–37.* DOI: [10.1002/tesq.157](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.157)

Abstract (captured from publisher page): 34 studies, 43 effect sizes, N = 3,942. Medium effect
(d = 0.46) for group contrasts and larger (d = 0.71) for pre–post contrasts. Conclusion:
extensive reading improves reading proficiency and should be part of language curricula.

## Bryfonski & McKay (2019) — TBLT implementation and evaluation: A meta-analysis
*Language Teaching Research, 23(5), 603–632.* DOI: [10.1177/1362168817744389](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168817744389)

Abstract (captured from publisher page): 52 studies of long-term task-based language teaching
programs in authentic classrooms. Overall positive and strong effect (d = 0.93) on a variety
of learning outcomes; positive stakeholder perceptions; moderators include program region,
institution type, needs analysis, cycles of implementation.

## Loewen, Crowther, Isbell, Kim, Maloney, Miller & Rawal (2019) — Mobile-assisted language learning: A Duolingo case study
*ReCALL, 31(3), 293–311.* DOI: [10.1017/S0958344019000065](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344019000065)

Abstract (captured from publisher page): 9 participants learned Turkish on Duolingo for a
semester (~29 h average). Improvement on L2 measures; positive moderate correlation between
time-on-app and gains; learners liked flexibility and gamification but reported variable
motivation and frustration with instructional materials. (Context from the LLT 2024 replication:
gains skewed toward reading/writing over listening/speaking; only one participant reached a
passing grade on a first-semester university Turkish exam.)

## Sung, Chang & Yang (2015) — How effective are mobile devices for language learning? A meta-analysis
*Educational Research Review, 16, 68–84.* DOI: [10.1016/j.edurev.2015.09.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2015.09.001)

Highlights/abstract (captured from publisher page): 44 studies, 1993–2013, N = 9,154. Overall
mean effect size d = 0.55 for mobile-device-assisted language instruction vs comparison
conditions; moderators (learning stage, hardware, software, setting, method, duration, skill,
target language) all examined; authors stress design quality and curricular integration.

## Lyu, Lai & Guo (2025) — Effectiveness of Chatbots in Improving Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis of Comparative Studies
*International Journal of Applied Linguistics, 35(2), 834–851.* Open Access.
DOI: [10.1111/ijal.12668](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijal.12668)
(Wiley blocks automated PDF download; full text captured from the publisher page 2026-08-22.)

- 31 studies, 41 effect sizes, N = 2,943. Overall **g = 0.608** (Z = 6.77, p < .001) — medium effect
  of chatbots on L2 learning. Heterogeneity high (I² = 80.75%), random-effects model.
- Affective outcomes g = 0.645; learning-performance outcomes g = 0.590; difference n.s.
- **Significant moderators:**
  - *Mobility*: chatbots with a mobile version **g = 0.790** vs non-mobile **g = 0.189** (Q = 17.63, p < .001).
    Largest single moderator in the analysis.
  - *Modality*: voice-input chatbots **g = 0.809** vs text-only **g = 0.425** (p < .05).
  - *Gen-AI powered*: **g = 0.833** vs rule/retrieval-based **g = 0.473** (p < .05).
  - *Comparison group*: vs traditional instruction **g = 0.841**; vs peer interaction **g = 0.645**;
    vs other non-interactive tech (apps, websites, Padlet) only **g = 0.323**. This is the honest
    benchmark — against a decent existing app, a chatbot's marginal gain is small.
- **Non-significant moderators**: educational level, FL vs SL context, individual vs collaborative,
  in-class vs beyond-class, customized vs generic chatbot, and **intervention duration**
  (meta-regression Q = 1.57, p = .21).
- Publication bias: funnel plot symmetric; fail-safe N = 2,554; Begg's test n.s.
- Average intervention duration across studies: 7.4 weeks (SD = 4.76). Mostly EFL (28/31 English),
  mostly adults (73%), mostly East Asia.

## Norris & Ortega (2000) — Effectiveness of L2 Instruction: A Research Synthesis and Quantitative Meta-analysis
*Language Learning, 50(3), 417–528.* DOI: [10.1111/0023-8333.00136](https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136)
(Wiley paywall; abstract captured from the publisher page 2026-08-22. No open-access copy located.)

Abstract: systematic research synthesis and meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental
studies of L2 instruction published 1980–1998. Comparisons of average effect sizes from **49
unique sample studies** indicated that (a) focused L2 instruction results in **large
target-oriented gains**, (b) **explicit types of instruction are more effective than implicit
types**, and (c) Focus on Form and Focus on FormS interventions result in **equivalent and large
effects**. Effects of instruction are **durable**. Type of outcome measure likely affects the
magnitude of observed effectiveness. Generalizability limited by inconsistent operationalization
across the field.
